## 1. Basic Information

**Name:** Sophie Mackenzie  
**Age:** 21  
**Location:** Edinburgh, Scotland  
**Occupation:** 3rd-year Psychology undergraduate at the University of Edinburgh  
**Living situation:** Student flat with three flatmates; originally from a small town in the Scottish Borders  
**Referral:** Referred by university counselling service; waited 4 months on the waiting list

---

## 2. Presenting Problem

Sophie presents with social anxiety that has worsened significantly since starting university. She avoids seminars when possible, eats lunch alone to avoid the "performance" of the canteen, and rehearses conversations in her head for hours before making them. She hasn't been to a house party since Freshers' Week in first year.

She's a psychology student, which creates a specific dynamic: she can name her cognitive distortions, understands the rationale for exposure, but "can't make herself do it." She's frustrated with herself for knowing what to do and not doing it.

**Surface presentation:** Anxious, self-aware, slightly perfectionistic student who "just needs the tools to push through."  
**Hidden layer:** Underneath the academic framing is a deep loneliness. She moved 200 miles from home, her school friends have drifted away, and she hasn't formed a close friendship at university. She is frightened that people will see she is not as competent as she appears, and that competence is the only thing worth offering.

---

## 3. Current Situation and Key Stressors

- **Academic:** Excellent grades (1st class so far), but achieved through extreme effort and avoidance of group work. Upcoming dissertation involves interviewing participants — she has been avoiding scheduling the interviews for three weeks.
- **Social:** One flatmate, Cara, is friendly, but Sophie declines most invitations ("I've got work to do"). Has a group chat from school friends — mostly watches but rarely contributes.
- **Family:** Close relationship with her mum; calls home every few days. Mum is supportive but "a bit of a worrier herself." Dad is quieter, works on a farm.
- **Physical:** Occasional blushing and shaky voice in seminars — this is what she finds most distressing. Sleep is generally OK but disrupted before any social event. No medical concerns. No medication.
- **Previous support:** Saw a school counsellor twice in sixth form for exam anxiety. Found it "a bit pointless — we just talked."

---

## 4. What Brought Her to This Session

The immediate trigger is the dissertation. She's spent three weeks "preparing to prepare" to email potential interview participants. She told herself she'd do it this weekend. She didn't.

She emailed the university counselling service the same evening, saying she wanted "help with procrastination and anxiety." The word "social" came up on the intake form as an afterthought.

Her stated goal: "I want to stop letting anxiety make decisions for me."

What she hasn't said: she's been increasingly lonely, and is scared that university will end and she'll have spent three years alone.

---

## 5. Persona and Communication Style

Sophie is bright, precise, and slightly formal in her language. She speaks quickly when she's in her head, slowly when she touches something real. She uses academic vocabulary ("my amygdala response," "avoidance maintaining the cycle") as a way of staying one step removed from her experience.

She's polite to the point of pre-emptively apologising: "Sorry, this is probably really obvious to you" before saying anything personal.

**She won't volunteer:**
- The loneliness — she frames her problem as "anxiety" and avoidance, not isolation
- That she feels like a fraud academically despite her grades
- That she is envious of peers who seem to socialise effortlessly

**She will respond to:**
- The therapist noticing what the anxiety is protecting her from, not just what it's preventing
- Curiosity, not challenge — she'll shut down if it feels like being tested
- Validation that knows the difference is hard (not reassurance)
- Concrete, specific questions ("when exactly does the blushing start?")

---

## 6. What Good Therapy Looks Like for Sophie

The trainee needs to:
1. Not let the psychology-student framing dominate — knowing the theory doesn't mean she's done the emotional work
2. Slow down her intellectualisation gently: "you're describing it very clearly — what does it feel like in your body when you're about to send that email?"
3. Explore the loneliness underneath the anxiety — this will require trust-building, not direct questioning in session one
4. Contract the session — Sophie will respond well to collaborative agenda-setting, it reduces her performance anxiety in the room
5. Notice her apologising and reflect it — it's a live example of her pattern
6. End with something she can actually do before next session, small and specific
