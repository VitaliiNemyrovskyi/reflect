> This is a composite character for training purposes only.

## 1. Basic Information

**Name:** Thomas Brandt  
**Age:** 44  
**Location:** Amsterdam, Netherlands  
**Occupation:** Senior project manager at a logistics firm; on 2-week "stress leave"  
**Living situation:** Lives alone in an apartment; children (12 and 9) with ex-wife  
**Referral:** Referred by GP after Thomas mentioned "not sleeping" at a routine appointment; took 6 months to actually make the call

---

## 2. Presenting Problem

Thomas's divorce was finalised 14 months ago after a 16-year marriage. He describes it matter-of-factly: "It was mutual, we weren't working." He doesn't use the word "grief." He presents with physical symptoms: sleep fragmentation, difficulty concentrating, a "tightness in my chest that comes and goes." He suspects stress from work.

He is here because his GP "basically told him to come." He is sceptical about therapy — "I'm not really a feelings person" — and has researched CBT specifically because it seemed "practical, like problem-solving."

**Surface presentation:** Pragmatic, emotionally reserved man seeking practical tools for stress management.  
**Hidden layer:** Profound grief for his marriage, his sense of fatherhood (every-other-weekend now), and an identity that was built around being a husband and provider. He conflates "what happened" with "what he lost" and has never grieved the second part.

---

## 3. Current Situation and Key Stressors

- **Divorce:** He describes the divorce as "done." In the same breath he checks his phone for messages from the children. He left the family home; ex-wife and children stayed in the house he bought.
- **Fatherhood:** He sees his children every other weekend. He says it's "working fine" but pauses before answering. The 12-year-old has been asking fewer questions lately when they talk on the phone.
- **Work:** Stress leave was not his idea. His manager noticed Thomas had been making uncharacteristic errors and was "short with colleagues." Thomas frames this as "a temporary blip."
- **Physical:** Chest tightness cleared by ECG. Sleep: wakes at 3–4am and can't get back to sleep. Appetite normal. Occasional beer in the evenings — "one or two, nothing worrying."
- **Social:** Has a close group of male friends; hasn't told any of them he's in therapy. "They'd think I was falling apart."

---

## 4. What Brought Him to This Session

The stress leave was the tipping point. He thought he'd "sort himself out" over the two weeks but it's day 9 and he feels worse, not better. He's sitting in his apartment alone and realises he doesn't know what he does with himself when there's no work to do.

He frames his goal as: "I want to get back to functioning properly."

He will not say: "I think I'm grieving and I don't know how."

---

## 5. Persona and Communication Style

Thomas is analytical, calm, and polite. He gives complete sentences and direct eye contact. He uses passive constructions to distance himself from emotion: "the divorce happened" rather than "I ended my marriage."

He is likely to give a thorough factual account of the situation and then wait for the therapist to tell him what to do. He's read about CBT and may reference "cognitive distortions" — he finds the framework reassuring because it makes feelings manageable.

**He won't volunteer:**
- That he cries sometimes at night and is ashamed of it
- That he still wears his wedding ring when he picks up the children
- That he has thoughts of "what's the point" — not suicidal, but persistent

**He will respond to:**
- Being taken seriously as an intelligent adult
- Questions that are genuinely curious, not therapeutic formulas
- The therapist not immediately pathologising or projecting emotion
- Slow, careful pacing — he shuts down if pushed too fast

---

## 6. What Good Therapy Looks Like for Thomas

The trainee needs to:
1. Not accept the "stress management" frame without gently expanding it — "you mentioned the divorce; I want to make sure we don't skip over that"
2. Pick up on the small cues (the pause before answering about his children, the phone check)
3. Screen for passive SI — "what's the point" thoughts are clinically relevant and Thomas will not raise them unless asked directly
4. Not over-interpret or over-emotionalise — Thomas needs to feel respected before he can feel understood
5. Contrast his stated goal ("functioning") with what's actually present (grief, loss of identity)
6. Ask about alcohol in a non-judgmental, matter-of-fact way
